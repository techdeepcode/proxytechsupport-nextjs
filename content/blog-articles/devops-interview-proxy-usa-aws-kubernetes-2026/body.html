DevOps Interview Proxy USA in October 2026

The U.S. hiring market in late 2026 is selective rather than frozen. Our companion guide covers the macro numbers in depth; the short version for DevOps engineers is that demand for operational skill is concentrated and strong even while overall hiring is cautious. When employers are careful with headcount, the interview bar rises and each loop gets more skeptical — and for infrastructure roles, “skeptical” means hands-on. Companies have learned that a candidate can recite the difference between a readiness and a liveness probe and still cause a self-inflicted outage in week two, so they test the behaviour, not the definition.

Three things make DevOps loops distinctively hard in 2026:

1. The surface area keeps expanding. A single “DevOps” title now spans cloud (usually AWS, often Azure or GCP), containers and Kubernetes, infrastructure-as-code, CI/CD, observability, and an increasing amount of security and FinOps. No one is expert in all of it, so interviewers probe depth in your lane plus judgment everywhere else.

2. The tools moved under everyone’s feet. Kubernetes shipped three releases in the last year and is on v1.37; EKS added 1.37 in October; Terraform’s licensing split birthed OpenTofu, now a CNCF project on its 1.13 line; supply-chain security went from nice-to-have to table stakes with signed artifacts and SLSA provenance. An engineer who stopped reading release notes in 2024 will sound dated in a 2026 loop.

3. Interviews are increasingly operational simulations. The strongest signal a company can get is watching you debug. Expect a shared terminal or a described incident, and expect to be judged on method: do you establish impact first, read the right signal, form one hypothesis at a time, and decide whether to mitigate or diagnose? That is a learnable, rehearsable skill — and it is precisely what matched preparation through DevOps proxy interview support drills.

That is the gap DevOps Interview Proxy USA closes: not teaching you DevOps from scratch, but compressing the distance between what you can already do and what an interviewer can observe of it in forty-five pressured minutes.

AWS DevOps Interview Proxy USA

AWS is the centre of gravity for most U.S. DevOps loops. The interviews that go well are rarely trivia quizzes (“what port does RDS use?”); they are judgment conversations where you defend choices against failure, blast radius and cost. AWS job support and matched AWS interview proxy support rehearse exactly these decisions. Here is the terrain, and how strong candidates handle it.

IAM and least privilege

IAM is where many AWS interviews start, because it reveals how you think about security. Be fluent in the difference between identity policies and resource policies, how a trust policy lets a role be assumed, and why Condition keys (such as aws:SourceArn or aws:PrincipalOrgID) are what actually scope a permission down. The senior move is to reason about the confused-deputy problem and to prefer short-lived credentials (role assumption, IRSA/Pod Identity for EKS) over long-lived access keys. A common prompt: “a Lambda needs to read one S3 prefix and write to one DynamoDB table — write the policy.” The weak answer grants s3:*; the strong answer scopes actions and resources precisely and explains why.

VPC networking, routing and security groups

Expect to reason about public versus private subnets, the role of route tables, NAT gateways for egress, and the difference between stateful security groups and stateless network ACLs. A frequent design question is how a private workload reaches AWS APIs without traversing the internet — the answer is VPC endpoints (interface/Gateway), and the follow-up is the cost and DNS implications. If you can draw a three-tier VPC and explain where a packet goes and what could silently drop it, you are ahead of most candidates.

EKS, ECS and the container decision

You will be asked when to choose EKS versus ECS/Fargate. The honest answer is a trade-off: ECS/Fargate is lower operational overhead and great when you want AWS to own the control plane and node lifecycle; EKS is the right call when you need the Kubernetes ecosystem (Helm, operators, portability, a platform team’s existing tooling). Know that EKS Auto Mode now manages compute, scaling and core add-ons for you, and that from Kubernetes 1.37 new Auto Mode node pools default their consolidation policy to Balanced, trading a little cost for fewer pod evictions. Being able to name that nuance signals you are current. Deep-dive help is available via the AWS EKS job support and ECS/Fargate pages.

Lambda and event-driven infrastructure

For serverless, interviewers probe cold starts, concurrency limits and the cost model, plus event sources (SQS, EventBridge, S3 notifications) and idempotency. The reasoning they want: when is event-driven the right shape (spiky, decoupled, bursty) and when does it become a distributed-debugging nightmare? Strong candidates mention dead-letter queues, partial-batch failures from SQS, and the importance of idempotent consumers because at-least-once delivery means duplicates are normal, not exceptional.

Load balancing, high availability and scaling

Know the ALB/NLB distinction (layer 7 vs layer 4), how target groups and health checks work, and how to design for an Availability Zone failure — spread across at least two or three AZs, and make sure your data tier and your autoscaling both tolerate losing one. On scaling, be ready to compare reactive scaling (target-tracking on CPU or a custom metric) with scheduled scaling for known patterns, and to discuss why scaling on the wrong metric (CPU when the bottleneck is a downstream queue) makes things worse.

Infrastructure as code, observability and cost

IaC is assumed, not optional — you will be expected to describe your Terraform or CloudFormation/CDK workflow. On observability, CloudWatch metrics, logs and alarms are the baseline; the senior signal is talking about the right signals (latency, errors, saturation) rather than a wall of dashboards. On cost, show awareness of the obvious levers (right-sizing, Graviton, Savings Plans, S3 lifecycle, NAT-gateway and cross-AZ data charges) and the judgment to trade cost against reliability deliberately rather than by accident. See cloud job support for the production equivalents.

Worked AWS scenario — a resilient, cost-sane ingestion pipeline

Prompt: “Ingest millions of device events per hour, process them, and store results durably. Defend every managed-service choice on reliability and cost.”

Edge:     API Gateway or an NLB  -> buffer, don't couple producers to consumers
Buffer:   Kinesis Data Streams OR SQS
            - Kinesis: ordered, replayable, multiple consumers, shard math & cost
            - SQS: simpler, cheaper, at-least-once, no ordering guarantee
Compute:  Lambda (spiky, low-ops) vs ECS/Fargate (steady, long tasks, cheaper at scale)
Store:    S3 (raw, cheap, lifecycle to Glacier) + DynamoDB/Aurora for queryable state
Fail-safe: DLQ on every async hop; idempotent writes; multi-AZ by default
Cost trap: cross-AZ + NAT data charges and over-provisioned Kinesis shards

The interviewer does not care whether you pick Kinesis or SQS. They care that you named the real trade-off (ordering/replay vs simplicity/cost), designed for at-least-once delivery, and put a dead-letter path on every async hop. That reasoning is the muscle these rounds exist to test.

Kubernetes Interview Proxy — EKS, AKS and GKE

Kubernetes is the single most interviewed DevOps technology in 2026, and the questions have matured past “what is a pod.” Managed control planes — EKS on AWS, AKS on Azure, GKE on Google Cloud — mean the interview focus has shifted to workload behaviour, reliability and troubleshooting. Matched practice is available through Kubernetes interview support and the Kubernetes job support guide.

Pod scheduling, requests and limits

Understand how the scheduler places pods: requests drive scheduling and the Quality-of-Service class (Guaranteed, Burstable, BestEffort), while limits drive throttling and OOM-kills. The classic trap: set no requests and pods pack onto a node until it is starved; set limits too low and your container is OOMKilled under load. Kubernetes 1.37 made in-place pod resize GA for init and sidecar containers, so you can now adjust CPU/memory of a running sidecar without recreating the pod — a genuinely useful, current detail to mention.

Readiness, liveness and startup probes

This is the most commonly mishandled topic and therefore a favourite interview filter. A liveness probe failing restarts the container; a readiness probe failing removes the pod from Service endpoints but does not restart it; a startup probe protects slow-booting apps from premature liveness kills. The single most important thing to say: a misconfigured readiness probe (passing before the app is truly ready, or failing transiently) causes self-inflicted 503s during rollouts — which is exactly Walkthrough A below.

Deployments, rollouts and Services/Ingress

Know what a rolling update actually does (maxSurge/maxUnavailable), how to roll back (kubectl rollout undo), and why a bad image plus an aggressive rollout can take down 100% of traffic without a canary step. On networking, be clear on ClusterIP vs NodePort vs LoadBalancer Services, how Ingress (or the newer Gateway API) routes external traffic, and how DNS resolves inside the cluster.

HPA, cluster autoscaling, RBAC and workload identity

Explain the Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (scaling on CPU, memory or custom/external metrics) and how it interacts with the Cluster Autoscaler or Karpenter adding nodes — and the failure mode where pods are Pending because HPA wants more replicas but no node has room. On security, RBAC (Roles/RoleBindings, least privilege for service accounts) and workload identity — IRSA or EKS Pod Identity on AWS, workload identity federation on GKE/AKS — let pods assume cloud permissions without static secrets. That is a frequent senior question.

Helm, GitOps and production troubleshooting

Helm for templating and release management; GitOps (Argo CD or Flux) for declarative, auditable delivery where Git is the source of truth and the cluster continuously reconciles to it. Expect to debug live. The commands below are the ones you will actually type — know what each output means and where it misleads:

# Is the rollout healthy, and are "Running" pods actually serving?
kubectl rollout status deploy/web -n prod
kubectl get pods -n prod -l app=web -o wide     # READY (e.g. 0/1) is truer than STATUS=Running

# Why is a pod not Ready? Read the probe and recent events.
kubectl describe pod <pod> -n prod | grep -A4 -E 'Readiness|Liveness|Events'
kubectl get events -n prod --sort-by=.lastTimestamp | tail -20

# Why Pending? Almost always scheduling: resources, taints, or no node room.
kubectl describe pod <pod> -n prod | grep -A6 Events   # look for "0/3 nodes are available"

# What changed, and can I roll back fast?
kubectl rollout history deploy/web -n prod
kubectl rollout undo deploy/web -n prod

The limitation to state out loud: kubectl get pods showing Running tells you the kubelet started the container, not that your application is healthy — the READY column and the readiness probe are the real signal. Candidates who know that one nuance consistently out-perform candidates who know ten more commands. Related deep dives: pod Pending troubleshooting and OOMKilled troubleshooting.

Terraform and Infrastructure-as-Code interviews

Infrastructure-as-code interviews test whether you can change production infrastructure safely. The tooling landscape is itself a current talking point: HashiCorp relicensed Terraform to the BUSL in 2023, which prompted the community fork OpenTofu — now a CNCF project that shipped 1.13.0 on 30 September 2026 with built-in state encryption, early variable evaluation, provider for_each, an -exclude flag and OCI registry support. Terraform itself continues in the 1.1x line. Knowing this split (and being neutral about it) signals you follow the ecosystem. Matched help: AWS Terraform job support and the troubleshooting walk-through at Terraform state locking & drift.

State management, remote state and locking

State is the heart of every Terraform interview. Explain why state exists (mapping config to real resources), why it must live in remote storage (an S3 bucket or equivalent) for any team, and why state locking (via DynamoDB, or S3-native locking in newer setups) prevents two applies from corrupting it. Be ready to discuss what a stuck lock looks like and how to release it safely with force-unlock — and why you only do that when you are certain no apply is running.

Modules, environment separation and dependencies

Good answers describe reusable modules with clear inputs/outputs and separate state per environment (dev/staging/prod) via workspaces or, more commonly at scale, separate state backends and directories. Interviewers like to probe dependency management: how Terraform builds its dependency graph, when you genuinely need depends_on versus relying on implicit references, and why over-using depends_on creates brittle plans.

Drift, plan/apply discipline and secrets

Drift — when the real world diverges from state because someone changed something in the console — is a favourite. The method: terraform plan (or a drift-detection run) surfaces it; you then decide to reconcile by importing/updating config or by reverting the manual change. On secrets, the strong answer is to keep them out of state where possible (use a secrets manager and data sources, or OpenTofu’s state encryption) and never to commit terraform.tfstate to Git, because state can contain plaintext secrets.

Worked IaC failure — a destructive plan after a provider upgrade

Scenario: you bump the AWS provider major version, run plan, and Terraform now wants to destroy and recreate a database and several security groups. Applying blindly would cause an outage and data loss.

# 1. NEVER apply a plan you don't understand. Read WHY each change is forced.
terraform plan -out tfplan
terraform show -json tfplan | jq '.resource_changes[]
  | select(.change.actions | index("delete"))
  | {addr:.address, actions:.change.actions}'

# 2. Isolate the blast radius with a targeted plan while you investigate.
terraform plan -target=aws_db_instance.main

# 3. Common root causes:
#    - a renamed/changed attribute in the new provider forces replacement
#    - a resource was recreated out-of-band; state no longer matches
#    - a default changed upstream (e.g. an argument became ForceNew)

# 4. Safe recovery, depending on cause:
#    - move/rename in state instead of recreating:  terraform state mv ...
#    - import the real resource so state matches:     terraform import ...
#    - pin the provider, upgrade deliberately, read the provider CHANGELOG

The interview (and the real job) reward the same instinct: a forced destroy/recreate of a stateful resource is a stop-the-line event. You read the plan, pin the provider, and reach for state mv/import before you ever type apply.

Migration and disaster recovery of state

Round this out with state-level DR: your state backend should be versioned and backed up (S3 versioning), because a lost or corrupted state file is far more painful than lost infrastructure — you can rebuild resources, but reconstructing the mapping is slow. Be able to describe migrating state between backends with terraform init -migrate-state and recovering a prior state version from backup.

CI/CD and DevSecOps Interview Proxy

CI/CD rounds test whether you can ship safely and prove what you shipped. The 2026 twist is supply-chain security: it is no longer enough to build and deploy; you must be able to show the artifact is the one you built and nobody tampered with it. DevOps job support and CI/CD pipeline failure troubleshooting cover the production side in depth.

The tools: GitHub Actions, Jenkins, GitLab CI and Argo CD

Know the model behind each. GitHub Actions — workflows, jobs, runners, reusable/composite actions, and OIDC to assume a cloud role instead of storing long-lived keys. Jenkins — declarative pipelines, agents, and why many teams are migrating off it but still run it at scale. GitLab CI — stages, the .gitlab-ci.yml model, and integrated registry. Argo CD — pull-based GitOps delivery that continuously reconciles the cluster to Git; Argo CD 3.x signs its own images with cosign and ships SLSA L3 provenance, a good example to cite when asked about trusting your tools.

Deployment strategies and rollback

Be fluent in rolling, blue/green and canary, and — more importantly — in the rollback criteria you define before you deploy: roll back automatically if the post-deploy health gate fails for N minutes, if error rate burns the error budget past a threshold, or if a critical-path smoke test fails. “We watch it and roll back if it looks bad” is a junior answer; naming objective, pre-agreed triggers is a senior one.

Supply-chain security, secrets and image vulnerabilities

This is the fastest-rising CI/CD interview area. The concepts to hold:

  • Provenance & signing. Sign build artifacts and container images (cosign/Sigstore) and generate provenance to the SLSA framework so a deployer can verify how an artifact was built. GitHub’s attest-build-provenance action produces this via Sigstore.
  • SBOMs. A software bill of materials lists what is in your image, so when the next critical CVE lands you can answer “are we affected?” in minutes, not days.
  • Secrets. No secrets in code or image layers; use OIDC-issued short-lived cloud credentials in CI, and a secrets manager at runtime. A leaked long-lived key in a public image is a classic post-mortem.
  • Image scanning & admission control. Scan images in the pipeline (Trivy/Grype) and enforce policy at the cluster edge with an admission controller (e.g. an OPA Gatekeeper or Kyverno policy) so an unsigned or critically-vulnerable image never gets scheduled.

Worked scenario — a failed production release

Scenario: the pipeline is green on every PR and the build passes, but the production deploy fails at the final gate with a non-obvious error. What is different between where it passes and where it fails?

Interrogate the ENVIRONMENT, not the code, in order of likelihood:
  1. Credentials: OIDC token / registry auth / cloud role present in CI
     but missing or expired in the prod deploy context.
  2. Artifact identity: is the pipeline deploying the image it just
     built, or a cached/older tag? (immutable, digest-pinned tags fix this)
  3. Config: a variable set in staging but unset in prod.
  4. Permissions: the CI principal can build+push but lacks deploy rights.
  5. Health gate: deploy waits on a readiness gate that never goes green
     -> the APP is the real failure, surfaced by the pipeline (see Walkthrough A).

The judgment call the interviewer is listening for: distinguishing a pipeline failure (infra/auth/config) from an application failure merely surfaced by the pipeline. The fixes are completely different, and conflating them wastes an outage.

DevOps system-design interview scenarios

DevOps system design is less about drawing boxes and more about defending trade-offs. The interviewer gives you an open-ended prompt and watches how you handle scale, failure, security and cost. Below are the scenario types that recur in U.S. senior loops, with the trade-off each is really testing. For matched practice at this level, see platform engineer interview proxy support and the SRE-flavoured reasoning in our SRE interview & production reliability guide.

ScenarioThe core trade-off it tests
Multi-region application deploymentActive-active vs active-passive: lower latency and higher availability against the real cost of data replication, consistency and failover complexity. Name your RTO/RPO before you pick.
Kubernetes platform architectureOne big multi-tenant cluster (cheaper, shared blast radius) vs many clusters (isolation, operational overhead). Namespaces + RBAC + policy vs hard cluster boundaries.
Highly available CI/CDA pipeline that cannot deploy is an availability problem too: redundant runners, no single credential/secret SPOF, and the ability to ship a fix when your own CI is degraded.
Disaster recoveryBackup/restore vs warm standby vs multi-region active-active — a straight cost-versus-RTO curve. The senior move is to tie the choice to a business RTO/RPO, not to pick the fanciest option.
Autoscaling & capacity planningReactive (HPA/Karpenter) vs predictive/scheduled scaling; headroom for spikes vs steady-state cost; scaling on the correct signal rather than CPU-by-default.
Logging, metrics and tracesCompleteness vs cost and cardinality. High-cardinality labels and verbose logs are where observability bills explode; sampling and retention tiers are the levers.
Security & identity boundariesWorkload identity and least privilege vs developer velocity; where to put the trust boundary; how blast radius is contained when (not if) a credential leaks.
Cost vs reliabilityThe meta-trade-off behind all the others. Every nine of availability costs money; the skill is making that trade deliberately and tying it to what the business actually needs.

A concrete example worth rehearsing out loud: “design a highly available deployment pipeline for a service that must be releasable 24/7, even during a regional cloud incident.” A strong answer reasons about where the pipeline’s own single points of failure are (the runner fleet, the artifact registry, the one person who holds the deploy credential), keeps the artifact registry and state replicated, uses digest-pinned immutable images so a degraded registry cannot serve the wrong bits, and defines a “break-glass” manual path for when the automated system itself is down. The technology names matter far less than showing you thought about failing the pipeline, not just the app.

The areas engineers most often need authorized external help with:

  • AWS and Azure environment and permission issues
  • Terraform deployment and state errors
  • Kubernetes production incidents (EKS/AKS/GKE)
  • CI/CD pipeline failures and flaky builds
  • Docker and Helm configuration problems
  • Observability gaps (metrics, logs, traces)
  • Infrastructure migrations and upgrades
  • Production deployment debugging and rollback
  • Security configuration and cloud networking

The diagnostic workflow is the real product. Good job support does not just hand you a fix; it teaches a repeatable method you can run yourself next time. For almost any infrastructure incident the shape is the same: (1) establish impact and scope — who/what is affected and how badly; (2) read the signals — the right metric, the recent change, the events and logs, not a random dashboard; (3) form one hypothesis at a time and test the cheapest-to-check first; (4) decide mitigate-vs-diagnose — while users are hurting, mitigation (roll back, shift traffic, scale out) usually wins and diagnosis follows on a non-serving replica; (5) prevent recurrence — the better probe, the canary step, the alert on the correct signal.

Confidentiality and authorization come first

Legitimate job support operates inside your employer’s rules. That means no sharing of cloud credentials, state files, kubeconfigs or proprietary source beyond what policy permits, no unauthorized system access, and respect for employment and client agreements that may restrict third-party involvement. The safest, most durable pattern is guidance on method and reasoning — how to approach the problem on your own authorized environment — rather than handing over confidential material. Redact account IDs and secrets from any shared logs. If your contract prohibits outside help, that boundary is absolute.

Walkthrough A — A Kubernetes Deployment is Running but returning HTTP 503

Business/technical impact. A checkout service begins intermittently returning 503s during a routine deploy. Some requests succeed, some fail — the worst kind of outage, because dashboards look “mostly green” while revenue leaks.

System context. A Deployment behind a Service and an Ingress, rolling out a new image with maxSurge=25%, maxUnavailable=25%. Pods show STATUS=Running.

Symptoms. 503s correlate with the rollout window; they are intermittent, not total; kubectl get pods shows pods Running but some with READY 0/1.

Investigation sequence.

# 1. Running != Ready. Check the READY column first.
kubectl get pods -n shop -l app=checkout -o wide

# 2. Why aren't they Ready? Read the readiness probe and events.
kubectl describe pod <pod> -n shop | grep -A4 -E 'Readiness|Events'
kubectl get events -n shop --sort-by=.lastTimestamp | tail -20

# 3. Does the Service actually have healthy endpoints?
kubectl get endpoints checkout -n shop        # are addresses present, or empty?

# 4. Correlate with the rollout timeline.
kubectl rollout history deploy/checkout -n shop

Evidence & root-cause possibilities. The most likely cause is a readiness-probe misconfiguration: the new pods pass readiness too early — before the app has warmed its connection pool or reached a dependency — so the Service routes traffic to pods that are not truly ready, producing intermittent 503s. Other candidates to rule out: the probe path/port is wrong after a config change; the app genuinely fails to start a dependency (DB, cache) and readiness correctly stays false while the rollout drains the old, working pods; or maxUnavailable is too aggressive, removing healthy pods faster than new ones become ready.

Safe recovery. Mitigate first. Roll back to restore users (kubectl rollout undo deploy/checkout -n shop), then diagnose on a non-serving replica. Do not keep debugging while the checkout path bleeds.

Prevention. A readiness probe that reflects true readiness (dependencies reachable, pool warmed); a conservative maxUnavailable and a canary step so a bad image never meets 100% of traffic; and an alert on Service 5xx rate, not just pod status.

Interviewer evaluation criteria. Did you distinguish Running from Ready immediately? Did you check endpoints? Did you choose to mitigate before diagnosing? Did you name the readiness-probe nuance? Those four moves separate senior from mid-level.

How this applies to real project work. This exact pattern is one of the most common EKS/AKS job-support calls. The method — Running≠Ready, check endpoints, mitigate-then-diagnose — is identical whether you are in an interview or on-call. See Kubernetes SRE & observability job support.

Walkthrough B — terraform apply fails after a state or provider change

Business/technical impact. An infrastructure change is blocked: terraform apply errors out, and because this stack manages networking and a database, nothing downstream can ship until it is resolved.

System context. Remote state in S3 with DynamoDB (or S3-native) locking; a recent AWS provider major-version bump and a teammate who “fixed something in the console last week.”

Symptoms. One of: an Error acquiring the state lock; a plan that wants to destroy/recreate live resources; or an apply that fails mid-way leaving state partially updated.

Investigation sequence.

# 1. Is it a lock problem? Who holds it, and is an apply really running?
#    Only force-unlock when you are CERTAIN nothing is applying.
terraform force-unlock <LOCK_ID>          # last resort, not first

# 2. Is the plan destructive? Read WHY before doing anything.
terraform plan -out tfplan
terraform show -json tfplan | jq '.resource_changes[]
  | select(.change.actions | index("delete")) | .address'

# 3. Did reality drift from state? Refresh-only to see.
terraform plan -refresh-only

# 4. Scope your investigation; don't apply the whole thing.
terraform plan -target=aws_db_instance.main

Evidence & root-cause possibilities. Three common roots: (a) the provider upgrade changed a default or made an argument ForceNew, so Terraform now wants to replace a resource that did not actually change; (b) the out-of-band console edit created drift, so state no longer matches reality and Terraform plans to “correct” it destructively; (c) a half-applied change left a stuck lock or a tainted resource.

Safe recovery. Never apply a destructive plan you do not understand. Depending on cause: pin the provider back, read its CHANGELOG, and upgrade deliberately; reconcile drift with terraform import (bring the real resource into state) or terraform state mv (fix an address change) instead of recreating; release a stale lock only after confirming no apply is live; and always have a versioned state backup to roll back to.

Prevention. Pin provider versions and bump them intentionally; forbid console changes to Terraform-managed resources (or run scheduled drift detection and alert on it); enable S3 versioning on state; and gate apply behind a reviewed plan in CI so a destructive diff is caught by a human.

Interviewer evaluation criteria. Do you refuse to apply a destructive plan blindly? Do you reach for import/state mv rather than recreate? Do you understand why state and locking exist? This round is really testing discipline under pressure.

Walkthrough C — a GitHub Actions pipeline succeeds, but the production deployment fails

Business/technical impact. CI is green, the team believes the release went out, but production is still running the old version — or worse, is now partially broken. The gap between “pipeline passed” and “change is live and healthy” is where silent failures hide.

System context. A GitHub Actions workflow builds an image, pushes it, and triggers a deploy (a kubectl apply/Helm upgrade, or an Argo CD sync) to an EKS cluster using OIDC to assume a deploy role.

Symptoms. The workflow reports success; production shows the old image digest, or new pods are not Ready, or the Argo CD app is OutOfSync/Degraded.

Investigation sequence.

# 1. What did CI actually deploy? Compare the built digest to what's running.
#    "latest" tags lie; compare immutable digests.
kubectl get deploy web -n prod -o jsonpath='{.spec.template.spec.containers[0].image}'

# 2. Did the deploy step really wait for health, or fire-and-forget?
#    A missing `kubectl rollout status` makes a broken deploy look "successful".

# 3. If GitOps: is Argo CD synced and healthy, or stuck?
argocd app get web          # Sync status + Health status
kubectl describe pod <new-pod> -n prod | grep -A6 Events   # ImagePullBackOff? CreateContainerConfigError?

# 4. Permissions & identity: did the OIDC role have deploy rights in prod?
#    Build+push rights != deploy rights.

Evidence & root-cause possibilities. Likely roots: (a) the pipeline deployed a mutable tag and the cluster pulled a cached/older layer — the build and the deploy disagree on what “this version” is; (b) the deploy step did not rollout status/wait, so a failing rollout never failed the job; (c) ImagePullBackOff because the cluster’s node role cannot pull from the registry, or CreateContainerConfigError from a missing secret/config in prod only; (d) the OIDC-assumed role can push images but lacks permission to update the Deployment.

Safe recovery. Confirm the running digest vs the intended one; if the new version is unhealthy, roll back (previous Helm revision, kubectl rollout undo, or Argo CD rollback) before investigating. Then fix the real gap — pin immutable digests, add a real health gate, or correct the IAM/registry permission.

Prevention. Deploy by immutable digest, never a floating tag; make the deploy job wait on rollout health and fail if it is not green; separate and least-privilege the push vs deploy identities; and for GitOps, alert on Argo CD OutOfSync/Degraded rather than trusting the CI checkmark.

Interviewer evaluation criteria. Do you understand that “green pipeline” ≠ “healthy production”? Do you compare digests rather than tags? Do you know a deploy step must wait for health to be meaningful? This is a senior-signal round precisely because juniors trust the checkmark.

How should engineers prepare for DevOps incident-response interview scenarios?

Rehearse a repeatable method rather than memorising fixes. For any incident prompt: establish impact and scope first, read the signals (metrics, events, logs, recent changes), form one hypothesis at a time, and decide explicitly whether to mitigate first (roll back, shift traffic) or diagnose first — mitigation usually wins while users are hurting. Narrate the trade-offs out loud, name what you would check next and why, and close with prevention (better probes, canary steps, guardrails, alerts on the right signal). Interviewers score the reasoning and communication, not whether you guess the exact root cause.
